Social protection

If you work informally in Africa, there is nothing underneath you when life goes wrong.

A pregnancy, an illness in the planting window, a failed harvest, a funeral, old age. For a smallholder farmer, each of those is carried by the household alone, or not at all.

The gap

Most of a continent is uncovered.

These are not our figures. They are the arena we work in, and they are the reason this programme exists.

19.1% of Africa’s population

Covered by at least one social protection benefit, against 52.4% worldwide. Coverage moved only from 15.2% in 2015.

85.8% of employment in Africa

Informal. No payslip, no employer of record, and no deduction anyone can collect.

Income once a year

A maize farmer is paid in one window. Food, school fees, inputs, medicine and funerals arrive in all twelve months.

No way to assess a contribution

A scheme cannot onboard somebody whose earnings nobody can see. That is an administrative problem, not an affordability one.

Social protection coverage and informality: International Labour Organization, World Social Protection Report 2024 to 2026, and ILO statistics on informal employment. These figures describe the sector, not DNC.

Why the order matters

Three things carry the fourth.

Most attempts at this start by selling a pension to somebody who cannot afford one. We think the order is wrong. Finance before planting raises the yield. Infrastructure means more of it is kept and sold well. That is what makes a contribution affordable, and then it repeats. A protected household plants the next season from a stronger base. It is a cycle, not a transfer.

How it would fit together

Four layers, and one transaction at the centre of them.

The farmer authorises a specific, revocable, fixed deduction from the proceeds of a defined crop sale. The deduction reduces what the buyer already owes for the crop, which is what keeps the structure clean for a regulator. The buyer never holds the farmer’s money.

Why this is the piece that is missing

A record is the thing a scheme cannot work without.

The reason social security has not been extended to the informal economy across most of Africa is not that nobody wants it. It is that you cannot track an income you cannot see, and if you cannot see an income you cannot set a contribution against it. A verified transaction history makes a farmer identifiable, locatable and income-visible, which is exactly the gate that enrolment has always failed at.

There is precedent. Contributions have been withheld at the crop sale in Brazil since 1991, and at the point of sale across cocoa in Ghana since 2024. When farmers in Zambia were asked how they would prefer to contribute, deduction by the buyer at the point of sale was chosen over mobile money, over headmen and over clinics.
