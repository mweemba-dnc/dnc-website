Insight · Smallholder credit

Smallholder lending asks neighbours to enforce repayment. In Malawi, a lender's memory did the work.

Farmers respond strongly to being remembered.

Home / Insights / Identification and repayment

13 November 2026 | ANALYSIS | 7 min read

In short. For forty years, the standard answer to lending without collateral has been to make borrowers responsible for each other. The best experimental evidence says that answer is weaker than its reputation. Removing group liability from a large Philippine lender left repayment unchanged. In rural Malawi, a different and far less celebrated change, making each farmer reliably identifiable to the lender, raised repayment among the riskiest borrowers from 67 percent of the loan to 92 percent. The lesson for anyone financing smallholders is that farmers respond strongly to being remembered. A loan system that cannot tell one borrower from another has switched off the most powerful repayment incentive it has.

The idea everyone copied

Group liability is one of the most widely copied ideas in development finance. The logic is elegant. A lender cannot see who is reliable, so it hands that job to the borrowers. Members choose each other, watch each other, and cover for each other, because if one defaults the group pays. Peer screening replaces the credit file. Social pressure replaces collateral.

The idea is so embedded that it is rarely tested. It was tested in the Philippines. Xavier Giné and Dean Karlan worked with Green Bank to run two randomised trials: one removed group liability from existing borrowing groups, the other assigned new villages to either group or individual loans. Groups kept meeting weekly in both. The result, published in the Journal of Development Economics in 2014: no increase in default in the short run or the long run, and the converted centres grew larger because individual loans attracted new clients.1

That finding does not settle the question everywhere. In India, Carpena, Cole, Shapiro and Zia studied an NGO that switched in the other direction, from individual to group liability, and found missed instalments fell by about 6 percent under group contracts.2 Group liability has an effect in some settings. What the Philippine trial shows is that it is not the indispensable mechanism it is usually taken to be, and that the same repayment can be achieved without making neighbours each other's guarantors.

If peer pressure is not doing most of the work, something else is.

What changed in Malawi

In 2007 and 2008, Giné, Jessica Goldberg and Dean Yang ran an experiment with paprika farmers in four districts of central Malawi: Dedza, Mchinji, Dowa and Kasungu, across the border from Zambia's Eastern Province.3 About 3,200 farmers in 214 farming clubs applied for input loans from the Malawi Rural Finance Corporation, with the crop sold at harvest to the buyer Cheetah Paprika, which passed the proceeds to the lender for repayment.

Every farmer heard the same talk about why a good credit history matters for future borrowing. Half were then fingerprinted as part of their loan application. They were told the print would identify them on any future application, and they watched a computer pick out one of their peers from a scan.

Nothing else changed. Same loan, same crop, same buyer, same terms.

For most farmers, repayment did not move. They were already repaying. For the farmers the researchers had predicted, from baseline characteristics, to be the most likely to default, the change was large. Two months after the due date, fingerprinted borrowers in that group had repaid 92 percent of their loan, against 67 percent in the comparison group. Fingerprinting closed roughly three quarters of the gap to full repayment, and nearly doubled the share of the riskiest borrowers who repaid in full on time.4

The farmers changed how they borrowed and how they farmed, not only how they paid. High-risk borrowers who were fingerprinted chose smaller loans. They put about 8 percentage points more of their land into paprika, the crop that repaid the loan, close to a 50 percent increase, and were less likely to sell or barter away the inputs they had borrowed. Fingerprinting did not reduce the number of farmers who chose to borrow. The researchers estimated a benefit to the lender of 2.34 times the cost of the system.4

Why identification works

The mechanism has a name in the economics literature: dynamic incentives. A borrower repays today because doing so keeps the door open tomorrow. The promise of a future loan, and the threat of losing it, is the cheapest enforcement a lender has.

But dynamic incentives work only if the lender can recognise the borrower next season. Where identity is weak, a farmer who defaults can reapply under a different name, or with a different lender, and the threat evaporates. The farmer knows this. So does the lender, which is one reason lenders in these markets ration credit and plan for the worst borrower, a cost that falls on the reliable farmers as much as the unreliable ones.

Seen this way, the two experiments tell one story. Group liability is a way of creating accountability when the lender cannot hold any. Identification gives the lender its own memory. Where a lender can remember, it needs neighbours to police each other less.

That last sentence is our own reading, drawn from two separate studies rather than one that tested both. The Malawi result is about farmers, seasonal loans and crop-linked repayment, the conditions that matter most for rural Zambia. The Philippine result is about urban microenterprise borrowers repaying weekly. Neither study ran both treatments side by side.

The Zambian gap

Zambia's smallholders mostly sit outside any lender's memory. The World Bank's last published Doing Business data put private credit bureau coverage at about 9 percent of Zambian adults in 2019, the final year the series ran.5 A smallholder who has borrowed in kind from a buyer, repaid from a harvest, or settled with an input supplier, has typically left no record that the next lender can read.

That has two consequences, and the Malawi experiment speaks to both. The farmer who repaid gets no credit for it. And the lender cannot make a credible promise of a future loan, because it has no reliable way to connect this season's borrower to next season's applicant. The most effective repayment incentive in the evidence base is unavailable, and the market falls back on rationing, collateral it cannot get, or group arrangements whose value is more modest than their reputation.

A fingerprint is not the only way to build that memory. What the Malawi study isolated is not the biometric, it is the borrower's belief that the lender will know who she is next year. A verified identity number, a consistent registration, a lender that records each loan against the individual and lets that record decide the next loan: any of these can create the same belief, provided the record is accurate and the next loan is real.

The objection, and the answer

The strongest objection is that a record which follows a farmer can also punish her for a drought she did not cause. Malawi's riskiest borrowers responded to identification, but in a bad year a reliable farmer can default for reasons that have nothing to do with intent. A system that treats every shortfall as a black mark would cut off exactly the people credit is meant to help, at exactly the wrong moment.

The answer is in how the record is used, not whether it exists. A lender that knows who its borrowers are can also tell a district-wide shortfall from an individual one. A verified history makes it possible to reschedule rather than refuse when a whole area is hit, and to keep a farmer's good seasons visible alongside a bad one. Memory without judgement is a blacklist. Memory with judgement is a credit history.

There is a second, quieter condition. In Malawi, the buyer collected the crop and passed proceeds to the lender, so repayment was attached to the sale. Identification raised repayment inside a structure that already made repayment easy. Lenders who rely on identification alone, with no link to the harvest, should not expect the same effect.

What DNC takes from this

DNC lends farmers cash during the season, repaid in maize at harvest, and records every loan and purchase against the individual farmer through its platform, Panjiia. The Malawi evidence is a reason to treat that record as the core of the lending model rather than a reporting by-product. Repayment is attached to the harvest, as it was in Malawi. The record is what lets the next season's loan depend on this season's behaviour, for the farmer and for any lender who later relies on it.

It also sets a standard. A record only creates the incentive if farmers know it exists and believe it will be used fairly. That means telling farmers plainly that their history is being kept and what it is for, building the distinction between a shock and a choice into how a missed repayment is treated, and making the next loan a real consequence of a good record.

For funders and lenders weighing smallholder credit in the region, the practical question this evidence raises is simple. Before asking how a programme will enforce repayment, ask whether it can recognise its borrowers next year. If it cannot, it has given up its strongest tool before the first loan is made.
